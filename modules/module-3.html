Module III · Estimated time: 2.5 hours

Ministering to Experiencers

Walking Alongside Congregants With Encounters of Their Own

Learning objectives: By the end of this module, you will be able to (1) respond pastorally to a congregant who discloses a personal UAP or NHI encounter without pathologizing or dismissing them, (2) recognize when an experiencer's disclosure calls for pastoral care alone versus when it calls for a referral, and (3) navigate the social tension that can arise in a congregation when one member's experience becomes known to others.

1. Why This Module Is Different

The first two modules dealt with questions. This one deals with people who tell you something happened to them. That is a different pastoral situation, and it deserves to be treated as one.

In this study, five of the twelve pastors interviewed had ministered directly to a congregant, or were themselves, someone who identified as having had a personal encounter. Their accounts converged on a pattern worth naming up front: the initial disclosure is almost always the most fragile moment. How you respond in that first conversation shapes whether that person ever brings anything spiritual to you again.

2. The Fear-to-Love Arc

Across the experiencer accounts in this research, a consistent shape emerged. The initial experience was almost always processed first through fear, confusion, or a sense of destabilization. Over time, for the pastors and congregants who had support and space to process it, that fear tended to give way to something more settled, often described in terms of a deepened rather than diminished faith.

This matters pastorally because your job in the early conversations is not to resolve the meaning of the experience. It is to walk with the person through the fear stage without rushing them toward a conclusion, whether that conclusion is "this was demonic" or "this was nothing" or "this was profound." Both premature dismissal and premature spiritual drama can interrupt a process that, given room, tends to move toward integration.

"As I shifted toward a foundation of love versus fear, the spiritual and supernatural experiences began to shift as well." Protestant Pastor, Study Participant

3. What Not to Do First

Two responses showed up repeatedly in the research as ones that damaged trust when they came too early, before the pastor had actually heard the full story.

Naming the category before hearing the whole story. Whether the conclusion is demonic, angelic, a hoax, or something else entirely, offering that interpretation in the first minutes of a disclosure, before you understand what the person actually experienced, tends to foreclose the conversation. It is not that any particular theological conclusion is off the table. It is that arriving at one before you have listened puts the interpretation ahead of the person, and congregants in this study consistently described feeling unheard when that happened, regardless of what conclusion their pastor landed on.

The clinical redirect. Immediately suggesting the person see a therapist or psychiatrist, without first offering pastoral presence, can communicate that you've decided their experience was a symptom before you've heard them out. This does not mean referral is never appropriate. It means referral should follow listening, not replace it.

What worked instead, according to the pastors who navigated this well: slow down, ask questions before offering interpretation, and let the person set the pace of how much theological or clinical weight gets attached to what they experienced. You may well land on a demonic interpretation, or any other interpretation, later in the conversation. The research suggests that landing there is far less damaging than arriving there first.

4. When to Refer

Pastoral presence is not a substitute for clinical care when clinical care is warranted, and part of ministering well here is knowing the difference.

Consider a referral, alongside continued pastoral support rather than instead of it, when:

  • The experience is accompanied by significant sleep disruption, functional impairment, or ongoing distress that isn't easing with time and support
  • The person describes symptoms that could indicate a mental health condition unrelated to the specific experience, and you don't have the clinical training to assess that
  • The person is isolating from support systems, including your congregation, because of the experience
  • Your own discernment tells you this has moved beyond what pastoral care alone can address

A referral offered with care, "I want you to have every kind of support available, and I think a counselor could help alongside what we're doing here," lands very differently than a referral offered as an exit from the conversation.

5. The Social Tension Question

Several pastors in the study described a second-order challenge: once an experiencer's story becomes known within the congregation, even inadvertently, it can create tension. Some members respond with curiosity or support. Others respond with suspicion toward the experiencer, or discomfort that their pastor is engaging with the topic at all rather than shutting it down.

This is a confidentiality and shepherding issue as much as a theological one. The pastors who handled it best treated the experiencer's story as pastorally confidential by default, the same way they would any other sensitive disclosure, and made their own calm engagement with the topic, from the pulpit if appropriate, in general and non-identifying terms, part of how they managed congregational anxiety rather than avoided it.

"You have to be willing to be labeled new age, an outsider, or on the fringe." Protestant Pastor, Study Participant

6. Staying Open

The final theme running through the experiencer accounts was a posture of ongoing openness rather than closure. The pastors who ministered well here did not treat the topic as something to be resolved once and filed away. They stayed willing to keep learning, keep listening, and let their own understanding develop as they encountered more of these conversations over time.

"I am still very open to the spiritual, but I take things with a grain of salt and test, discern." Protestant Pastor, Study Participant

7. Closing the Module

An experiencer sitting across from you is not a theological puzzle. They are a person who has had something happen to them that has likely destabilized their sense of the world, and they have chosen to bring it to you specifically, as their pastor, rather than to anyone else. That trust is the whole of what this module has been about protecting.

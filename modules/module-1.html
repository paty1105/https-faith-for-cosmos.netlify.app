Module I · Estimated time: 2.5 hours

Theological Grounding

Creation, Discernment, and the Deception Question

Learning objectives: By the end of this module, you will be able to (1) articulate a biblically grounded position on where UAP and nonhuman intelligence questions fit within a doctrine of creation, (2) name the specific discernment and deception concerns your congregants are likely already carrying, and (3) explain, in your own words, why silence is not a neutral pastoral option.

1. Why This Module Comes First

You cannot offer pastoral reassurance you have not first worked out theologically. Before we talk about what to say to a congregant, we have to talk about what you actually believe is true. That is the order this course follows on purpose. Twelve Protestant pastors sat down for this research, and the ones who ministered with the most steadiness on this topic were not the ones with the cleverest answers. They were the ones who had done the theological work in advance, so that when the question showed up in their office or after a service, they were not building a framework in real time.

This module is that framework-building work.

2. Room Enough in Creation

Scripture never promises us an exhaustive inventory of everything God has made. Colossians 1:16 tells us that all things were created through Christ and for him, visible and invisible, in heaven and on earth. That is a wide net. It does not specify what fills it.

The pastors in this study who held the most stability on this question tended to share a common move: they treated the possibility of nonhuman intelligence as a creation question before treating it as a threat question. If God's creative work extends to what is invisible to us, then the existence of intelligent life elsewhere in that creation is not, on its own, a doctrinal problem. It becomes a problem only when it gets tangled up with claims about salvation, revelation, or the uniqueness of Christ's redemptive work, questions this module will return to.

"The goal is not to limit God to what we have already known." Protestant Pastor, Study Participant
Reflection prompt: Before this research, where did you privately locate this question? Curiosity, threat, distraction, or something you had simply never let yourself think through? Write one paragraph. You will use it again in Module IV.

3. Human Limitation as a Theological Starting Point

Job 38 through 41 is God's answer to a man demanding explanations, and the answer is essentially a tour of everything Job does not understand and was never in a position to understand. Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth. The pastors in this study who were most comfortable saying "I don't know" to a congregant were, without exception, the ones who had made peace with the fact that Scripture itself models epistemic humility as a form of faithfulness, not a failure of it.

This matters because your congregants are not actually asking you to resolve the UAP question. Most of them already sense that nobody can. What they are asking, underneath the surface question, is whether their faith has room for genuine mystery, or whether admitting uncertainty on your part will make them doubt everything else you have taught them.

It will not, if you frame it correctly. Uncertainty about a specific phenomenon is not the same as uncertainty about the character of God. You can hold those two things with very different confidence levels at the same time, and part of your job in this module is getting comfortable naming that difference out loud.

4. The Deception Question

This is where most pastors in the study reported the most internal tension, and it is worth naming directly rather than softening it. Scripture is not silent on the possibility of deceptive spiritual activity. 2 Corinthians 11:14 warns that Satan disguises himself as an angel of light. Matthew 24:24 warns of false christs and false prophets performing great signs and wonders, sufficient to deceive, if possible, even the elect.

A pastoral posture that ignores this warning is not actually neutral. It is a quiet form of naivety that will eventually cost you credibility with the more discernment-minded members of your congregation. But a posture that leads with this warning, that treats every UAP question as a satanic deception on its face, tends to produce the opposite problem: it shuts down the congregant who came to you in good faith and now feels dismissed or spiritually pathologized for asking.

The pastors who navigated this best in the study held both things without collapsing one into the other: genuine biblical caution about deception, alongside genuine openness to the possibility that not everything unexplained is demonic. Discernment, not default suspicion, was the operative posture. That distinction becomes the practical center of Module II, but the theological groundwork for it belongs here.

"The goal is to help people think carefully, with theological tools in hand, rather than react emotionally." Protestant Pastor, Study Participant

5. Where This Intersects Science and Disclosure Narratives

Part of what makes this question harder than it looks is that it arrives from multiple directions at once: government disclosure hearings, scientific speculation about the scale of the universe, and personal testimonies from congregants, all landing on your desk as though they were one question. They are not. This module's final task is helping you separate them.

A congressional hearing about unidentified aerial phenomena is a claim about observed, unexplained physical objects. A scientist's estimate of probable life elsewhere in the universe is a claim about probability, not about specific encounters. A congregant's personal experience is a pastoral care situation first, regardless of what caused it. Treating all three as the same category of question is how well-meaning pastors end up either overcommitting to an explanation they cannot defend, or dismissing something that deserved a more careful hearing.

"My skeptical reflex is: it's political theater, made to distract from other things going on in the world." Protestant Pastor, Study Participant

6. Closing the Module

You are not being asked to resolve, in an afternoon, a question theologians and scientists have not resolved. You are being asked to know where you stand well enough that a congregant leaves your office more settled than they arrived, not because you gave them certainty you do not have, but because you gave them a pastor who was not afraid of the question.

  1. One sentence stating your theological position on where UAP/NHI questions fit in a doctrine of creation.
  2. One sentence naming your working position on the deception question, discernment versus default suspicion.
  3. One open question you are still sitting with. You are not expected to have zero.
